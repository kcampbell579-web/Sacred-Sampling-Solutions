Why test

Lead is the element that gets the attention, but it is rarely the only metal in household dust. Cadmium turns up from older pigments and some imported goods, chromium from certain paints and treated materials, arsenic from historical pesticide use and older pressure-treated timber.

Because the instrument reads the whole spectrum at once, adding elements is unusually cheap — which makes a four-element panel a better answer than a single-element test for anyone whose concern is general rather than specific. If your concern is specifically lead and a young child, the three-surface Lead Dust Wipe Kit is the better product: three locations beat four elements for that question.

How to collect your sample

This kit gives you breadth of analytes rather than breadth of locations, so pick the one surface where the answer matters most. A full square foot is preferable — more area means more collected mass and a more reliable result at low concentrations.

Do not clean before sampling. Place the template, wipe the full area to the pattern in the instructions, seal the wipe in its tube, and record the location, surface type and measured area. Every result is normalised to the area you record.

How the lab analyzes it

The wipe is acid-digested and the solution is introduced into an inductively coupled argon plasma running at several thousand degrees, which excites every element present. Each element emits light at its own characteristic wavelengths and the spectrometer reads all of them simultaneously, under EPA Method 6010D.

Results are reported per unit area based on the area you recorded.

What this test does not tell you
  • One wipe, one surface. This kit trades locations for elements and the report describes a single place.
  • Mercury is not included — it cannot run alongside the others and is available separately on request.
  • Only lead has a published surface clearance level. Cadmium, chromium and arsenic are reported as measured concentrations with no comparison value, and the report says so plainly rather than inventing a threshold.
  • It measures metals in settled surface dust — not in paint, water, soil or a consumer product.
