Why test

A consumer air monitor gives one TVOC number, and the health significance of that number depends completely on which compounds make it up — which the monitor cannot say.

This is the first measurement to take when a room smells wrong, when flooring or cabinetry has just gone in, or when one person in the household reacts to one particular space. It's a starting point by design — if formaldehyde is the concern, the Standard kit adds the badge that measures it.

How to run the badge

Use the included room-selection guide to pick the space you actually want to know about. Open the sealed pouch, write down the exact start time, and place the badge at breathing height (3–5 ft), in the middle of the room, away from vents, windows, purifiers and direct sun — nothing draped over it.

Leave it undisturbed for 8 to 12 hours while living normally. Seal it back in the pouch, record the end time, and post it the same day. Return within 14 days of use.

How the lab analyzes it

Activated charcoal behind a diffusion barrier collects organic vapour at a characterised rate. At the laboratory the charcoal is solvent-desorbed and run on gas chromatography with mass spectrometry, which separates the mixture and identifies each compound by its fragmentation pattern.

Each compound is reported as its own time-weighted average concentration for the period recorded. That speciation — naming each chemical instead of summing them — is the entire point of the product.

What this test does not tell you
  • A time-weighted average cannot show a spike.
  • The badge measures one room.
  • It does not measure formaldehyde — that needs DNPH chemistry and a different badge (see the Standard IAQ kit).
  • Methanol, methyl acrylate and vinyl chloride cannot be reliably collected. No U.S. residential VOC limit exists; the report gives measured values against reference points.
