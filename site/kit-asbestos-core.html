What it tests for
  • Presence or absence of asbestos in the material you submit
  • The asbestos type(s) identified — chrysotile, amosite, crocidolite and the other regulated minerals
  • The estimated percentage of asbestos in the bulk material
Why test

Asbestos in a wall is not a hazard. Asbestos in the air is. For anyone about to start work on an older building, the entire question is whether the thing they're about to cut into will put fibres into the air when they do.

Testing before disturbance is what determines whether a job is a weekend project or a licensed abatement — a decision with real cost attached in both directions, and not one anyone should make by looking at the material. One sample costs less than a box of drop cloths.

How to collect your sample

Wet the material first. Lightly misting the spot with water before you touch it is the single most effective thing you can do to keep fibres out of the air. Turn off forced-air heating/cooling, close the door, and put on the supplied gloves and N95 respirator.

Collect a small representative portion — roughly the volume of a large coin — taking it through the full depth of the material where practical. Depth matters more than quantity, because asbestos can be in one layer and absent from the next. Seal it in the container, double-bag it, wipe the area with a damp cloth, and return it in the prepaid mailer.

Do not sand, grind, drill, saw, sweep or vacuum to obtain a sample. If a representative sample can't be taken without significant disturbance, stop and use a licensed asbestos professional.

How the lab analyzes it

The laboratory examines the prepared material under a polarized light microscope. Asbestos minerals have distinctive optical behaviour — morphology, refractive characteristics, birefringence, extinction, sign of elongation — and the analyst uses that combination to identify which regulated types are present. Concentration is then estimated by calibrated visual assessment against reference standards, reported as a percentage under the applicable New York State ELAP method.

PLM is the standard first-line method for friable bulk materials worldwide — fast, inexpensive, and entirely adequate for a material that crumbles readily.

What this test does not tell you
  • A result applies only to the specific material submitted. Different materials, layers, colours, areas and manufacturing lots may each require their own sample — one negative does not establish a building is asbestos-free.
  • This method estimates concentration by calibrated visual assessment. Where a result is very close to the 1% regulatory threshold, or the material is unusual, electron microscopy gives a more precise answer — see the Advanced kit.
  • PLM is not the correct method for non-friable materials like vinyl tile, mastic and roofing — the binder masks the fibres and can return a false negative. Use the Standard kit for those.
  • This is a laboratory analysis of the sample submitted. It is not an asbestos survey or building inspection and does not satisfy any regulatory pre-renovation survey requirement.
