The Air Quality Index looks simple: a number, a color, a word. But that tidy scale is doing a lot of work behind the scenes, and knowing how it's built makes it far more useful.

## What the number actually measures

The AQI isn't one measurement. On any given day it reflects the **worst-performing** of several pollutants — usually ground-level ozone and PM2.5, sometimes others. The reported AQI is whichever pollutant is currently the most concerning, converted onto a common 0–500 scale.

## The colors, decoded

- **Green (0–50):** Good. Air quality poses little risk.
- **Yellow (51–100):** Moderate. Unusually sensitive people may notice effects.
- **Orange (101–150):** Unhealthy for sensitive groups.
- **Red (151–200):** Unhealthy for everyone.
- **Purple / Maroon (201+):** Very unhealthy to hazardous.

## When to trust it — and when to dig deeper

The AQI is a genuinely good tool, but two caveats matter. First, it's often based on the nearest monitor, which may be miles away during a fast-moving smoke event. Second, it's an average over time, so a calm daily number can hide a dangerous evening spike.

For a smoke event specifically, check the **PM2.5** value directly, not just the headline AQI, and treat "unhealthy for sensitive groups" as your cue to act if you're in one of those groups.

## The bottom line

A colored number is a starting point, not the whole answer. Read the pollutant behind it, know your own risk category, and you'll get far more out of the same free data everyone else glances past.
