When a workplace disaster kills many people at once, it leads the news. But most workplace deaths happen **one at a time**, in workplaces no camera ever visits — and the aggregate is staggering.

## The Fatal Four

Decade after decade, a handful of causes account for the majority of construction and industrial deaths:

- **Falls** — consistently the single largest category.
- **Struck-by** incidents — vehicles, falling objects, swinging loads.
- **Electrocutions.**
- **Caught-in / caught-between** — machinery, trenches, collapsing material.

The unsettling part isn't the variety. It's the repetition. The same four, in roughly the same proportions, year after year.

## Why "preventable" is the key word

Almost every one of these deaths maps to a known control that wasn't in place: a guardrail, a lockout procedure, a trench box, a moment of training.

> When the same failure kills people the same way every year, "accident" stops being the right word.

## What the headline skips

A viral safety story usually frames a death as a shocking one-off. The data tells a different story — of predictable hazards, understaffed inspection regimes, and economic pressure that pushes corners to get cut. Behind the Headlines exists to put the single event back into the pattern it belongs to.

## The takeaway

Workplace safety isn't about freak accidents. It's about whether known, cheap, effective controls are actually used — and who is accountable when they aren't.
