Two words explain a disproportionate share of catastrophic bridge failures: **fracture critical.** It's an engineering classification for a structure with no redundancy — where losing one key member means losing the entire span.

## What "fracture critical" means

In a redundant structure, if one member fails, load redistributes to others and the structure survives long enough to warn you. A fracture-critical member has no such backup:

- One tension member, one load path.
- If it fails, there is nothing to catch the load.
- Failure can propagate across the whole structure in **seconds**.

## Why so many exist

Fracture-critical designs were efficient — less material, lower cost. Under mid-century assumptions about loads, inspection, and steel behavior, they were deemed acceptable. Then traffic loads grew, steel aged, corrosion crept in, and inspection budgets tightened.

> Redundancy is expensive. Its absence is invisible — right up until the moment it isn't.

## The compounding factors

Rarely is it one thing. A slightly undersized connection detail, plus decades of added dead load from repaving, plus corrosion, plus a heavy day of traffic. Each factor alone survivable; stacked together, catastrophic.

## The stakes now

A large share of the bridges in daily use were built generations ago and are carrying loads their designers never modeled. Understanding fracture criticality isn't academic — it's the lens through which inspectors decide which structures get watched, retrofitted, or closed before they make the news.
