The "credit card a week" statistic went around the world. It's memorable, it's alarming — and it's built on a stack of assumptions worth examining. This episode is about what we actually know.

## What counts as a microplastic

By definition, a microplastic is any plastic fragment smaller than **five millimeters**. They come in two flavors:

- **Primary** microplastics, manufactured small — microbeads, industrial pellets.
- **Secondary** microplastics, the far larger category: bottles, bags, and textiles slowly shredding into fragments.

## Where your exposure actually comes from

Bottled water, synthetic textiles shedding in the wash, dust, and food packaging are among the most-cited routes. Tap water contains them too, though often at lower levels than bottled.

## What the science can — and can't — say

Here's the honest part. We can now *find* microplastics almost everywhere we look, including human blood and tissue. What we don't yet have is a clear dose-response picture for health effects in people. That uncertainty isn't a reason to dismiss the issue — it's a reason to fund the research and reduce needless exposure where it's cheap to do so.

> Absence of proof isn't proof of safety. But it isn't proof of harm either. Good science lives in that gap.

## Practical takeaways

Cutting single-use plastic, filtering tap water, and running a full sink of cold water rather than repeatedly reboiling can all trim exposure — without pretending the problem is solved.
